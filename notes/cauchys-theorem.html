Cauchy's Theorem

Why this theorem

One of the central results in complex analysis is Cauchy's Theorem, and it's also one of the results I found most striking. After years of studying mathematics, I had often encountered elegant theorems, but this one felt surprising and powerful in a different way. When I first came across it, the conclusion seemed almost too perfect — such a simple set of conditions leading to such a strong, elegant result. It stood out from many of the theorems I'd studied before, and that sense of surprise is what made me want to talk about Cauchy's Theorem in depth.

Prerequisites

There are several special cases of Cauchy's Theorem, depending on the contour surrounding the function. Think of a contour as a closed path in the complex plane, one that must be piecewise differentiable. Imagine moving your pen across the paper (our complex plane). You can draw straight lines or curves, but you can't lift your pen until the path closes. A contour is simple if it doesn't cross itself. Simple contours have an "inside," which is bounded, and an "outside," which is unbounded.

The type of function we're considering is what we call a holomorphic function. Holomorphic is just a fancy word for a complex differentiable function, mapping from a non-empty, open subset of the complex plane to the complex plane itself. We say a function is holomorphic "on and inside" \(\Gamma\) if we can find a region \(\Omega\) that contains \(\Gamma\) and its inside, on which \(f\) is holomorphic.

Here, I'll talk about the classical statement of Cauchy's Theorem, which applies to holomorphic functions surrounded by simple contours. In the last section, I'll briefly mention some of its many applications in complex analysis.

Cauchy's Theorem

Let \(\Gamma\) be a simple contour, and \(f\) be a complex function which is holomorphic "on and inside" \(\Gamma\). Then

\[ \int_{\Gamma}f(z)\,dz = 0. \]

It looks rather strange when you first see it. So — if I manage to find a region that contains my contour, on which \(f\) is holomorphic, I can just say the integral over that contour is... zero?

Why is this true?

The proof splits into two cases, and the easy one is genuinely easy. A region \(\Omega\) is called starlike if there's some point \(w_0\) inside it, called a starcentre, such that for every other point \(z\) in \(\Omega\), the straight line segment \([w_0, z]\) stays entirely inside \(\Omega\). In other words, from \(w_0\) you can "see" every point in the region without the line of sight ever leaving the region. If the region enclosed by \(\Gamma\) is starlike, the result follows almost immediately from a simpler, earlier version of Cauchy's Theorem. A disk is the classic example of a starlike region: pick the center as your starcentre, and every straight line to another point inside stays inside.

The more interesting question is what happens when the region isn't starlike, i.e., when the contour loops around in a way that no single point has a clear line of sight to the rest of the region. This is where the proof gets clever.

The idea is to cut the awkward region into several smaller pieces, using what are called cross-cuts, chosen so that each smaller piece is starlike. Once you've done that, you can apply the easy case to each piece individually, and each one gives you zero, by the starlike case above.

Now here's the part I find genuinely satisfying: when you add all those smaller integrals back together, every cross-cut gets traversed twice: once by each of the two sub-contours on either side of it, but in opposite directions. So the contributions from the cross-cuts cancel out exactly, term by term. What's left over is just the integral over the original contour \(\Gamma\), and since everything summed to zero, that original integral must be zero too.

It's not a fully rigorous proof. Pinning down exactly how to find the "inside" of an arbitrary contour, or exactly where to place the cross-cuts, gets fiddly in general. But in practice, for the contours you actually encounter, finding suitable cross-cuts is usually straightforward. What I like about this proof is the strategy more than the details: take a hard, general case, and break it into pieces simple enough that a result you already trust can carry the weight.
