How I learned to Think Combinatorially

Where I started

I used to think I wasn't a combinatorics person.

During my first year at university, combinatorics was probably the field of mathematics I struggled with most. A few years later, I chose to specialise further in that same subject.

This wasn't an isolated experience. I've noticed a recurring pattern in the things I enjoy and have become good at: they aren't necessarily the things that came naturally to me. In fact, it is often quite the opposite. Some of the subjects I am strongest in now are the ones I once struggled with the most.

I think part of it is that I really dislike being bad at something. When I encounter a subject I can't understand, I tend to spend more time on it—not because I enjoy struggling, but because I can't stand the feeling of not being able to do it. Eventually, that extra time can turn unfamiliarity into confidence, and confidence into something I actually enjoy.

Looking back, I noticed I was much more comfortable with the continuous side of mathematics—continuity, limits, and analysis. Combinatorics required a more discrete way of thinking, and that style of problem-solving initially felt unfamiliar to me.

I don't think the biggest change was simply learning more combinatorics. It was learning how to think combinatorially.

Why combinatorics frustrated me

One of the things that distinguished combinatorics from other areas of mathematics was that many elementary problems require surprisingly little advanced mathematical theory. This accessibility is part of what makes combinatorics so prevalent in mathematical competitions such as Math Olympiads.

You don't need to know an advanced theorem to solve a question about how many ways something can be arranged or how many different objects can be selected. But, for me, this made the subject more intimidating rather than less. When I looked at a counting problem involving thousands or millions of possibilities, I assumed that solving it required some kind of abstract combinatorial intuition that I simply didn't possess.

Problems in analysis often felt more structured. Take a familiar question such as proving that the sequence of some numbers converges to some other number. Once you understand what it means formally for a sequence to converge, you have a definition to work from. The problem may still require thought, but there is a framework guiding you. You simply start with the definition and work towards what needs to be shown.

Combinatorics felt different. I could encounter two questions that looked completely unrelated, even though they were ultimately solved using the same underlying idea. There were problems where I could understand every word of the question but still have absolutely no idea where to begin.

I think this was the source of much of my frustration. I couldn't yet see the underlying structure of a problem or recognise which technique or counting principle might apply. I was accustomed to a more definition-driven way of solving problems. In combinatorics, I often had to figure out the approach before I could even begin solving the problem.

At least for me, analysis and combinatorics therefore trained me to ask two different questions:

Analysis: What follows from these definitions?

Combinatorics: What structure is hiding in this problem?

To become better at combinatorics, I had to become better at spotting that structure. I had to learn to look at an unfamiliar problem and connect it to something I had encountered before: perhaps a similar counting argument, a bijection, an invariant, a recurrence, or simply a different way of representing the problem.

Learning to think differently

One of the things that changed my perspective was encountering a powerful idea:

Many counting problems can, in some sense, be viewed as problems of counting words.

Consider a simple question:

How many shortest paths are there from the bottom-left corner of a \(3 \times 3\) grid to the top-right corner, if you can only move right or upwards?

At first, this looks like a problem about paths through a grid. A naive approach would be to imagine and count all the possible paths one by one. If the problem considers a \(10 \times 10\) grid or even larger grids, then this approach quickly becomes impractical. To solve this in a much easier way, we observe that we can actually represent every path as a word.

Let \(R\) represent a move to the right and \(U\) represent a move upwards. Every shortest path therefore consists of three \(R\)'s and three \(U\)'s. For example,

\(RRUURU\)

represents one possible path.

The original geometric problem has now become a problem about counting six-letter words containing exactly three \(R\)'s and three \(U\)'s. We simply choose which three of the six positions contain \(R\)'s:

\[\binom{6}{3}=20\] possible paths.

The important step wasn't calculating \(\binom{6}{3}\). It was deciding that the paths should be represented as strings in the first place.

For the \(10 \times10\) case, we have \(\binom{20}{10}=184,756\) possible paths.

This way of thinking extends far beyond grid paths. Many combinatorial problems that initially look unrelated are really instances of familiar mathematical structures once we find the right representation.

Assigning three distinct roles to seven distinct people can be viewed as counting the number of functions from a 7-set to a 3-set (each person is mapped to one of the three roles) which we know to have a total of \(3^7\) possible functions.

The same idea appears outside elementary counting. In the early 18th century, the mathematician Leonhard Euler studied the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem, he represented the physical layout of the city as a graph: the landmasses became vertices and the bridges became edges. The problem was no longer really about bridges. It had become a problem about the structure of a graph. This study of graphs was later developed to become known as Graph Theory.

I think this is one of the most beautiful aspects of mathematics. A problem can look completely different depending on the language we use to describe it.

A path through a grid can become a word.

Assigning people to roles can become a function.

A collection of bridges can become a graph.

What matters is not just knowing these mathematical objects, but learning to recognise when a problem can be translated into one of them.

Of course, knowing the theory alone isn't enough. The only real way I found to develop this intuition was through practice. After seeing enough different problems, I gradually built a mental library of structures and connections. Some of them eventually became almost instantaneous: I could see a problem about assigning roles to distinct people and think, that can be about functions. A grid-path problem made me think of strings. Practice wasn't just making me faster at solving problems. It changed what I noticed when I looked at them.

Choosing combinatorics again

After months of wrestling with combinatorial concepts and working through dozens of practice questions, I went from feeling frustrated by combinatorics to genuinely enjoying the problems I encountered. I later chose to research Ramsey Theory, a subfield of combinatorics, for a second-year module, and eventually specialised further in combinatorics and graph theory in my third year.

Looking back, it is strange to think that a subject I once felt I wasn't naturally good at became one of the areas of mathematics I enjoy most. That has changed how I interpret mathematics:

Struggling with something doesn't necessarily mean that you aren't suited to it. Sometimes it means that you haven't yet learned how to see the problems in the way it requires.

For me, that was the difference between looking at a combinatorics problem and seeing a large collection of possibilities, and looking at the same problem and asking:

What structure is hiding here?
